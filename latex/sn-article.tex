\title[Rethinking Rare-Class ECG Evaluation]{Rethinking Rare-Class Evaluation for Inter-Patient ECG Arrhythmia Classification}
\author{\fnm{Van Thong} \sur{NGUYEN} [0009-0007-9360-9112]}\email{nguyenthong111206@gmail.com}
\author{\fnm{Khoi Nguyen} \sur{TRAN} [0009-0004-3486-1607]}
\affil*{\orgname{University of Economics Ho Chi Minh City (UEH)}, \city{Ho Chi Minh City}, \country{Vietnam}}

\abstract{
Rare-class performance in inter-patient ECG classification is highly sensitive
to patient composition. Conventional patient-disjoint evaluation can still
produce fragile point estimates when a rare-class test set is dominated by one
or two records. We study this problem with a record-grouped leave-patients-out
protocol for AAMI heartbeat classification, comparing a flat recurrent
classifier with a configuration that adds morphology and rhythm-related inputs.
The evaluation exposes substantial fold-level heterogeneity for fusion and
supraventricular beats that is hidden by a single conventional split. Forensic
analyses separate ranking, calibration, subtype composition, patient-relative
geometry, and decision-policy effects. The enhanced configuration improves
supraventricular/PAC ranking, but a pre-registered risk-controlled selective
policy does not achieve a useful operating point across reliable folds. Fusion
errors are consistent with label ambiguity or missing context in this dataset,
single-beat setting, available leads, and tested pipelines; accordingly, fusion
beats are framed as a referral class rather than a successfully automated class.
The main contribution is therefore an evaluation methodology and an honest
account of rare-class failure, not a claim that a new architecture solves the
rare classes.
}
\keywords{ECG arrhythmia classification, rare classes, inter-patient evaluation,
leave-patients-out validation, patient heterogeneity, selective prediction,
fusion beats, supraventricular beats}

\section{Introduction}
Heartbeat classification results depend not only on the model but also on the
unit used to construct the train and test partitions. Random beat-level splits
can leak patient-specific morphology, while even a fixed patient-disjoint split
can yield a fragile rare-class estimate when its test support is concentrated in
a small number of records. This distinction is especially important for the
AAMI supraventricular (S) and ventricular--normal fusion (F) classes.

This paper re-examines rare-class ECG evaluation using the inter-patient
DS1/DS2 protocol of de Chazal et al.~\cite{RN21} together with a record-grouped
leave-patients-out evaluation. The purpose is not to present F or S as solved,
but to determine which apparent gains persist across held-out patient groups
and which disappear under patient heterogeneity.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a record-grouped leave-patients-out protocol that exposes the
  patient-composition sensitivity hidden by a single conventional split;
  \item a controlled fold- and seed-level comparison of a flat recurrent
  baseline and the R2 configuration, without attributing the observed change to
  an untested individual component;
  \item a forensic analysis of S/PAC subtype composition and F errors using
  ranking, calibration, record composition, and patient-relative geometry; and
  \item a pre-registered selective-decision evaluation whose negative result is
  retained as evidence, leading to an operational referral framing for F.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Inter-patient ECG evaluation and the DS1/DS2 protocol}
The MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database~\cite{RN20} remains a common benchmark for
heartbeat classification. De Chazal et al.~\cite{RN21} established a widely
used patient-disjoint DS1/DS2 protocol. Mousavi et al.~\cite{ref29} and Zhou
et al.~\cite{ref30} also reported sequence-to-sequence and hybrid classifiers
under this split.
These studies are important comparators, but a shared split does not by itself
make a rare-class estimate representative when a small number of patients
provide most test examples. Our work focuses on that remaining evaluation gap.

\subsection{Transformer and edge-oriented ECG classifiers}
Recent CNN--Transformer and lightweight wearable-oriented models continue to
extend ECG classification~\cite{ref31,ref32}. Their evaluation protocols range
from patient-disjoint to intra-patient or stratified settings, so their headline
scores are not directly interchangeable. We therefore compare evaluation
designs explicitly rather than treating heterogeneous published scores as a
single leaderboard.

\subsection{Rare classes, calibration, and selective prediction}
Class imbalance~\cite{RN5}, patient-specific morphology, and uncertainty
estimation~\cite{RN19} complicate rare-class decisions. Calibration and
selective prediction can separate ranking ability from operational acceptance,
but their validity depends on the calibration unit and on distributional
stability. We evaluate realised fold- and patient-level behaviour rather than
assuming that a beat-level nominal target transfers to unseen patients.

\section{Record-Grouped Leave-Patients-Out Protocol}
\subsection{Datasets and AAMI label mapping}
MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database records form the primary evaluation source. Beats
are centred on the annotated R peak and mapped to the five AAMI groups N, S, V,
F, and Q. The principal morphology input is the MLII lead when it is available;
the R2 configuration additionally uses the available V1-channel signal. INCART
records provide supporting patient and morphology diversity, with lead II as
the principal morphology channel and the available V1 lead for the auxiliary
path. Because INCART and MIT-BIH differ in acquisition and population, all
results are stratified by source. MIT-BIH alone determines the primary
conclusions and the selective-prediction GO/NO-GO decision; INCART is supporting
evidence only.

Preprocessing preserves record identity through every stage. INCART signals
undergo source-specific baseline filtering, R-peak recentering, polarity
correction, and a morphology-quality filter before entering the combined pool.
No globally normalised waveform is stored. Normalisation is fitted afresh
inside each outer fold as described below, preventing information from an outer
test record from entering its training transformation.
\subsection{Conventional DS1/DS2 reference evaluation}
For literature continuity, we retain the patient-disjoint DS1/DS2 partition
introduced by de Chazal et al.~\cite{RN21}. DS1 supplies model development data
and DS2 is held out for evaluation. Although this protocol prevents the same
record from appearing on both sides of the split, its rare-class test support is
highly concentrated: 93\% of its F beats come from patient 213 and 75\% of its S
beats come from patient 232. Consequently, its F and S scores are sensitive to
the behaviour of those particular records. We use DS1/DS2 as a conventional
reference rather than treating its single rare-class estimate as a patient
distribution.
\subsection{Outer folds, inner validation, and conformal calibration}
The primary protocol is five-fold grouped cross-validation with the ECG record
as the statistical and splitting unit. No record contributes beats to more than
one partition within a fold. Records are ordered by their F and S beat counts
and assigned by a snake-draft procedure, first distributing F-bearing records,
then S-only records, and finally the remaining records. This deliberately
places the large rare-class record clusters in different outer folds rather
than allowing one fold to absorb all of them. The resulting fold values are
therefore reported individually as the empirical distribution over held-out
patient groups.

Each outer iteration contains three mutually record-disjoint development
partitions. The \emph{model-train} records fit the network parameters. The
\emph{inner-validation} records select the best epoch using macro-F1 over
\{S,V,F\}; the same frozen inner-validation predictions subsequently serve as
the tuning data for per-class temperature fitting. A separate
\emph{conformal-calibration} partition is carved only from S-bearing, F-free
records. This restriction protects the already small pool of F-bearing records
from being removed from model training and makes explicit that the conformal
procedure is evaluated for S, whereas F analysis is referral-diagnostic only.
None of these partitions contains an outer-test record, and no threshold,
temperature, epoch, or model parameter is fitted on the outer test fold.

Waveform mean and standard deviation are estimated separately for each database
source using model-train records only, then applied unchanged to model-train,
inner-validation, conformal-calibration, and outer-test beats. Training follows
the same recurrent recipe for the flat and R2 configurations. Best-epoch
predictions selected by inner-validation \{S,V,F\} macro-F1 are the primary
outputs. Tail-epoch stochastic weight averaging was also evaluated, with batch
normalisation statistics recomputed on model-train data, but it did not improve
R2 and is not used for the primary comparison.

Before committing GPU resources to all outer folds, candidate configurations
were screened on one fixed sentinel split containing difficult, high-support F
and S records from both databases. A candidate advanced to full two-seed LOPO
evaluation only if it improved a sentinel rare class without reducing N or V
relative to the preceding retained configuration. The sentinel was a screening
device only; all paper conclusions use the complete outer-fold evaluation.
\subsection{Statistical units and reporting}
Outer-fold values, not pooled beats, are the primary generalisation summaries.
The two training seeds for a given fold are paired repetitions on the same held-
out records and are not treated as independent patients. We report both seed
values, their within-fold mean and spread, and all five fold means. R2--flat
comparisons are paired by fold and seed. Patient-level uncertainty uses the
record as the resampling cluster, keeping all beats from a selected record
together; an independent-beat bootstrap is not used as primary uncertainty
evidence. Source-stratified MIT-BIH and INCART results remain separate throughout.

\section{Main Results}
\subsection{Patient-composition sensitivity of rare-class estimates}
% Artifacts: results/logs/baseline_results.json; results/lopo_cv/summary_flat.json.
For the same flat RLSTMClassifier model family, the conventional DS1/DS2
reference run obtained F1-F $=0.241$, whereas its MIT-BIH best-epoch fold mean
under record-grouped LOPO was $0.053$. We describe this as an observed
cross-protocol gap, not as a controlled estimate of the causal effect of the
split. The LOPO pipeline additionally introduced INCART records into the
development pool, used a shorter training schedule, and capped the number of N
training beats. These differences can also affect optimisation and learned
representations. What the comparison establishes directly is that the
conventional rare-class point estimate did not describe performance across the
held-out patient groups encountered by the matched LOPO model family.

The concentration documented in Section~3 explains why the discrepancy matters:
although DS1 and DS2 are record-disjoint, their F test estimate is dominated by
one patient. LOPO distributes the dominant rare-class records across outer folds
and exposes the resulting variability rather than compressing it into one test
number.
\subsection{Per-fold rare-class performance}
% Artifact: results/p1_gate/report.json.
Figure~\ref{fig:lopo_pairs} shows every paired seed result and emphasises the
mean within each outer fold. On MIT-BIH, R2 achieved a five-fold mean F1-F of
$0.070$, with fold means ranging from $0.000$ to $0.164$. Its mean F1-S was
$0.377$, with fold means ranging from $0.144$ to $0.507$. Thus neither class is
well represented by a single aggregate value: F effectively disappears in
three folds, while S performance changes substantially with the held-out record
group.

\subsection{Patient-level heterogeneity and uncertainty}
% Artifacts: results/p1_gate/report.json; results/p2_final/patient_level_metrics.csv;
% results/p2_final/patient_cluster_uncertainty.json.
The fold supports themselves are heterogeneous: MIT-BIH F support across the
five outer folds is 399, 388, 82, 72, and 42 beats, while S support is 388, 391,
576, 860, and 2,490. These counts are properties of held-out record groups, not
sampling noise that should be erased through beat pooling. Accordingly, the
primary tables retain all five folds and the two paired seeds. For the selective
analysis, uncertainty intervals resample whole records after averaging the two
model-seed measurements within each record. With only about nine MIT-BIH test
records in a fold, these intervals are necessarily wide and are interpreted
alongside, rather than instead of, the raw fold and patient values.

\section{HMR-MultiPath R2 Configuration}
\subsection{Flat recurrent baseline}
The flat comparator is the repository's RLSTMClassifier. A compact one-
dimensional convolutional frontend extracts local morphology, followed by a
two-layer bidirectional recurrent block built from Residual Memory Control
(RMC) cells. Attention pooling converts the recurrent sequence to a beat-level
embedding, and a shared fully connected head produces logits for the five AAMI
classes. The flat model is deliberately retained as the strongest matched
baseline: it uses the same outer folds, inner-validation rule, optimisation
recipe, class handling, evaluation protocol, and metric definitions as R2.
\subsection{R2 inputs and residual heads}
R2 preserves the entire flat morphology trunk and shared five-class head. It
adds two lightweight residual specialists. A small convolutional encoder of the
available V1-channel signal is concatenated with the shared beat embedding and
produces an additive correction to the F logit. A rhythm multilayer perceptron
encodes the stored beat-relative RR features and produces an additive correction
to the S logit. The final layers of both residual heads are zero-initialised, so
R2 begins with logits identical to the shared flat head and changes the F or S
logit only when the auxiliary input supports such a correction.

R2 is used here as a diagnostic configuration rather than presented as a new
architectural contribution. The full LOPO comparison contrasts the joint
V1-plus-rhythm R2 configuration with flat; it does not contain a full V1-only
ablation. Any observed S/PAC change is therefore attributed to the R2
configuration as a whole, not specifically to its rhythm branch.
\subsection{R2 versus flat under identical LOPO folds}
% Artifacts: results/p1_gate/report.json; results/forensic/E1_report.json.
R2 increased MIT-BIH F1-S by $0.145$ on average over the five paired fold means.
Four of five fold means did not decrease. Its average within-fold S seed spread
was $0.033$, compared with $0.036$ for flat. The two largest gains were also
directionally consistent across seeds: fold 1 improved by $0.235$ and $0.240$,
and fold 4 by $0.364$ and $0.469$. Fold 2 was the exception, with one negative
and one positive seed-level difference and a slightly negative fold-mean change.

The ranking evidence is more uniform than the F1 evidence. R2 S-AUC exceeded
flat S-AUC in every MIT-BIH fold: respectively $0.733$ versus $0.499$, $0.980$
versus $0.873$, $0.951$ versus $0.795$, $0.950$ versus $0.823$, and $0.816$
versus $0.485$. We therefore interpret R2 as a modest, real improvement in
S/PAC ranking. This does not imply that S is solved: the fold variability remains
large, and Section~7 tests whether the ranking gain supports an operationally
useful selective decision.

\section{Forensic Analysis of Fusion and Supraventricular Beats}
\label{sec:forensic}
\subsection{S/PAC ranking and subtype composition}
\label{sec:s-forensic}
% Artifacts: results/forensic/E1_report.json; results/forensic/E5_subtype.json.
Subtype composition does not explain the difficult S folds. The original MIT-BIH
symbol A accounts for between 85\% and 99\% of S beats in every fold. Conditioning
the ranking analysis on A therefore leaves all five folds eligible, yet R2's
MIT-BIH A-only AUC still ranges from $0.664$ to $0.978$, a spread of $0.314$.
This is wider, not narrower, than the aggregate S-AUC spread. In contrast,
INCART A-only AUC ranges from $0.923$ to $0.993$, a spread of $0.070$. These
results reject the registered hypothesis that changing proportions of AAMI-S
subtypes drive the MIT-BIH fold variance. They instead locate the instability
at the patient/domain level, while showing that PAC ranking can be strong in
other held-out record groups.
\subsection{Patient concentration in difficult S folds}
% Artifacts: results/forensic/E1_report.json; results/forensic/E5_subtype.json.
Fold 4 illustrates the danger of beat-weighted interpretation. Patient 232
contributes 1,382 of the fold's 2,490 S beats, or 55.5\%. A strong beat-level
score or selective operating point in this fold can consequently be driven by
one presentation. We retain fold 4 in all reported results but excluded it from
the pre-registered multi-fold GO count. Fold 0 is difficult for a different
reason: its S support is not similarly concentrated in one record, yet its
ranking AUC is only $0.733$. The two folds therefore should not be treated as
instances of a single subtype-mixture failure.
\subsection{Low-rank F errors}
% Artifact: results/forensic/E1_report.json.
Among true-F beats that R2 classified as N, 48.7\% fall in the bottom three
deciles of the model's F probability rank, whereas only 4.5\% fall in the top
three deciles. The main failure is therefore not a population of strongly
F-ranked examples narrowly losing the argmax. Moreover, ROC-AUC can look
moderate while precision--recall utility collapses: on fold 2, F AUC is $0.785$
but average precision is $0.001$ and F1-F is zero. This discrepancy reflects
the small F prevalence and large false-positive burden and prevents AUC alone
from being interpreted as usable F separability.
\subsection{Patient-relative F geometry}
% Artifact: results/forensic/E5_fgeometry.json.
Patient-relative geometry explains model behaviour more consistently than a
global F cluster. For each record, N and V centroids were constructed without
using F labels. Within the third of F samples nearest their patient's N centroid,
94--99\% were classified N, depending on representation and distance. Conversely,
F predictions became more frequent in the high-intermediacy tertile. The gradient
replicated in logits and in the pre-head embedding, and with cosine and Euclidean
distance, reducing the likelihood that it is solely an artefact of one geometry.

This mechanism did not yield evidence for patient adaptation. Across record--seed
pairs, the absolute Spearman correlations between patient isolation and F recall
or precision were only $0.12$--$0.23$, were descriptively non-significant, and
changed sign between logits and embeddings. We therefore retain the
patient-relative geometry observation but reject the actionable claim that
greater geometric isolation identifies patients for whom adaptation would
recover F performance.
\subsection{What the forensic evidence does not establish}
% Artifacts: results/forensic/E2_ceiling.md; results/forensic/E2_rule_proxy.json;
% results/forensic/E5_notes.md.
The public MIT-BIH release contains adjudicated consensus annotations rather
than the original independent label layers. Our literature review found no
F-specific inter-annotator agreement statistic. A transparent rule proxy reached
F1 $0.051$ and Cohen's $\kappa=0.022$, but this is a measurement of that rule
against consensus labels, not a human-agreement or Bayes ceiling. Its failure
could arise from crude measurements, missing leads or rhythm context, patient
variation, or ambiguous labels. Taken together with the low-rank errors and
geometry results, the evidence is consistent with label ambiguity or missing
context on this data and in the tested single-beat pipelines; it does not prove
that fusion beats are universally unclassifiable.

\section{Selective Prediction and Referral}
\subsection{Pre-registered decision policy and operating criterion}
For each fold and seed, a vector of class-specific temperatures is fitted on
inner-validation logits and then frozen. On the separate conformal-calibration
records, the S nonconformity score is one minus the calibrated probability of S
for true-S beats. Its split-conformal quantile defines an S-calibrated threshold
at each pre-specified miscoverage level. Applying that threshold to all class
probabilities produces a prediction set. A beat is automatically decided only
when the set is a singleton and its singleton class is not F; singleton-F and
all non-singleton or empty sets are referred. F-related quantities are therefore
referral diagnostics, never the result of searching for an ``optimal F
threshold.'' Realised S coverage on every outer fold is reported alongside its
nominal target because patient shift weakens the exchangeability assumption.

The useful-operating-point criterion was fixed before the final continuous
risk--coverage curves were inspected. For a MIT-BIH fold, we consider the point
at which the overall selective risk among all accepted automatic decisions is
at most 0.02. The fold has a useful S operating point only if, at that point,
the class-conditional auto-S coverage
$P(\mathrm{accept}\land\widehat{Y}=S\mid Y=S)$ is at least 0.15 and the auto-S
precision $P(Y=S\mid\mathrm{accept}\land\widehat{Y}=S)$ is at least 0.90.
The S selective contribution receives a GO verdict only if at least two folds
from the pre-registered ranking-reliable set \{1,2,3\} meet all three conditions.
Fold 0 is excluded because its S ranking AUC is 0.73
(Section~\ref{sec:s-forensic}), and fold 4 is excluded
because 55\% of its S support comes from one patient. These exclusions affect
the GO count but do not remove either fold from the reported results.

% Artifacts: results/p2_final/go_criterion.csv;

No ranking-reliable fold met the complete useful-operating-point criterion
(Table~\ref{tab:p2-go}). Fold 1 produced no accepted S decision in its safe
region. Fold 2 retained high auto-S precision ($0.964$) but only $0.026$
auto-S coverage, far below the required coverage. Fold 3 produced $0.379$
coverage but only $0.299$ precision. Fold 4 had no point with overall selective
risk at or below $0.02$; it would not have counted toward GO in any case. Thus
R2 achieved 0/3 qualifying reliable folds, and flat also achieved 0/3.

Temperature calibration is not the missing lever. R2's S classwise ECE improved
on folds 1 and 3, yet neither produced a qualifying point; on fold 2 it changed
slightly in the wrong direction. The flat model provides the stronger structural
control: its fold-2 S-ECE decreased from $0.092$ to $0.036$ while producing no S
singleton anywhere on the evaluated grid. Better average calibration therefore
did not translate into useful selective S automation under the registered rule.
\subsection{Referral framing for F}
% Artifacts: results/p2_final/f_referral_diagnostics.csv;
% results/forensic/E1_report.json; results/forensic/E5_fgeometry.json.
The selective layer receives a NO-GO verdict as an S automation contribution.
For F, it was never evaluated as a threshold-optimisation mechanism: every
singleton-F decision is referred by construction, and the reported F quantities
describe referral workload and leakage only. Combined with the low-rank F errors
and patient-relative geometry, this supports an operational recommendation to
refer F-labelled candidates in this dataset, single-beat setting, available
leads, and tested inter-patient pipelines. It is not a claim that all possible
fusion-beat classifiers or decision policies must fail.
