                                        Abstract
     Automatic ECG arrhythmia classification is now common in cardiac monitoring,
     but detecting rare and dangerous beat types is still difficult. LSTM-based models
     are popular yet struggle on several fronts: long-range dependencies across 187-
     sample heartbeats, class imbalance that reaches 113×, and no built-in way for a
     clinician to see what the model is doing. We present HMR-BiLSTM, a BiLSTM
     augmented with a Residual Memory Control (RMC) cell and a Hybrid Mem-
     ory Path. The RMC cell adds an interaction-aware residual gate on top of the
     standard LSTM gates and splits memory into keep and add streams weighted
     by learned softmax attention, while attention pooling weights computed after
     the recurrent loop provide a signal that can be overlaid on the ECG waveform
     for inspection. The Hybrid Memory Path runs the RMC cell alongside a con-
     ventional LSTM through a learnable blend gate, so the model behaves like a
     standard LSTM early in training and relies on the RMC pathway more as train-
     ing progresses. On MIT-BIH, the model reaches macro AUC 0.9916, macro
     Recall 0.9146, and macro F1 0.8825, with recall 4.43 percentage points above
     CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN and gains concentrated on the Fusion and Supraventricu-
     lar classes. Under FGSM at ε = 0.02, HMR-BiLSTM holds macro F1 at 0.842
     versus 0.773 for CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN; under PGD-20 it holds at 0.839 ver-
     sus 0.756. Across these tests, HMR-BiLSTM holds up better than the recurrent
     baselines, though we stop short of any deployment claim.

1 Introduction
Heartbeat classifiers now sit inside everything from hospital Holter monitors to con-
sumer smartwatches, and most still lean on a plain LSTM. That backbone has three
soft spots. Its forget gate multiplies element by element, so it never really models how
the cell state and the hidden state interact. Its gating tends to drift over the 187 sam-
ples of a beat, which bites hardest when normal beats outnumber the rarest class 113
to 1. And it says nothing about why a given beat was flagged. We did not treat these
as cosmetic: a ventricular beat that slips through can delay care, so we judged the
model not only on accuracy but on whether it holds up under noise, whether a cardi-
ologist can read its reasoning, and whether it survives small adversarial perturbations,
a test still rare in ECG work yet hard to dismiss once a model starts informing clinical
decisions. To tackle all three, we place a Residual Memory Control (RMC) mechanism
inside a standard BiLSTM cell, giving HMR-BiLSTM; on MIT-BIH under the AAMI
EC57 protocol it reaches macro AUC 0.9916 against 0.9803 for CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN
and lifts macro recall from 0.8703 to 0.9146. The paper contributes (i) an RMC cell
that adds an interaction-aware residual gate, a keep/add memory split, and softmax
attention over both memory streams to a standard LSTM; (ii) a Hybrid Memory Path
that blends the RMC cell with an ordinary LSTM through a learnable gate, steadying
training and raising minority-class recall under the 113:1 imbalance; (iii) attention-
pooling weights that overlay on the raw trace to give a per-time-step, clinician-facing
read of each decision; and (iv) a robustness and calibration study under FGSM, PGD,
and Gaussian noise, where the model keeps a recall-priority margin over recurrent
baselines on the rare, high-risk classes.


2 Related Work
ECG classification is a multi-class time-series problem made harder by severe class
imbalance [1]. Earlier work fed hand-extracted features (RR interval, QRS width, P/T-
wave amplitude) to a Logistic Regression [2] or a Decision Tree [3], which are easy
to follow but break under noise or population shift, whereas deep models drop that
step: on MIT-BIH, 1D CNNs [4] and BiLSTMs both do well, with Yildirim [5] hitting
99.39% accuracy and Hannun et al. [6] matching cardiologists across 91,000 patients.
Attention [7] and Transformer blocks [8, 9] later sharpened the focus on diagnostically
relevant segments, but the recurrent core itself was left largely untouched, even as
Greff et al. [10] mapped the LSTM design space and Zoph and Le [11] found searched
structures that outperform it, which is where our memory-augmented cell comes in.
Explaining these models is no longer optional once they reach the clinic: SHAP [12]
and LIME [13] rank feature importance and attention maps show where the model
looks [7, 8], but neither reveals how memory shifts across the P-wave, QRS, and T-wave

inside the recurrent loop, which is why Rudin [14] argues high-stakes medicine should
favour models interpretable by design, and clinical-readiness surveys keep returning
to the same shortlist of reliability, transparency, robustness, and calibration [15, 16].
That robustness concern is not hypothetical: Han et al. [17] evaded both CNN and
RNN ECG classifiers with minuscule perturbations, spurring ECG-specific defences
such as adversarial distillation [18], while Begoli et al. [19] cautioned that a model
with no sense of its own uncertainty should not be deployed alone. HMR-BiLSTM
addresses this gap with a lightweight mechanism inside the LSTM cell, paired with
attention-pooling weights that can be plotted next to the ECG signal for inspection.


3 Proposed Methodology
Scope of Contribution. HMR-BiLSTM keeps the foundational LSTM equations
intact and adds a specialised memory-routing mechanism, the RMC cell and Hybrid
Memory Path, targeting two failure modes of conventional LSTMs on long ECG
sequences: poor minority-class recall under extreme imbalance and the absence of
intrinsic time-step interpretability. The smoothness constraint and attention pool-
ing act as stabilisation and temporal-focus layers, while the core novelty is the
hybrid blending mechanism, which trades a modest parameter increase for better
minority-class recall and built-in inspectability.

3.1 Problem Formulation
Given an input ECG segment X = (x1 , . . . , xT ) with xt ∈ Rd , ∀t ∈ {1, 2, . . . , T } with
T = 187 and d = 1 for single-lead MIT-BIH signals, the goal is to learn a function
f : RT ×d → ∆K−1 that maps each segment to a probability distribution over K = 5
heartbeat classes following the AAMI EC57 standard [20] (N, S, V, F, Q). Training
uses Focal Loss with per-class weights:

We compute the modulating term (1 −pi,k )γ from the unweighted cross-entropy before
applying the per-class weight wk . This ordering differs from the original formulation of
Lin et al. [21], where α is folded into the focal term directly. Our variant is designed to
make the focusing mechanism (1 − pi,k )γ reflect the model’s raw prediction difficulty,
independent of the class prior encoded in wk . In practice the difference is small, but
we note it for reproducibility. Class weights follow the scikit-learn balanced formula
and are clipped to [0.5, 10.0].

3.2 HMR-BiLSTM Architecture
The architecture consists of three stacked blocks. The CNN frontend applies two
Conv1D–BN–ReLU–MaxPool blocks (kernel sizes 5 and 3, pooling stride 2), reducing
sequence length from T = 187 to T ′ = 46 while expanding channels from 1 to 64 to


                                               3
capture local waveform patterns before the recurrent block. The HMR-BiLSTM block
stacks two bidirectional LSTM layers (num layers = 2), the first layer’s hidden out-
put feeding the second; each cell uses the Residual Memory Control mechanism with a
Hybrid Memory Path (Sections 3.3–3.4), and the final hidden states are concatenated
across directions to dimension 2H = 192 before attention pooling. An attention pool-
ing layer then learns adaptive weights γt over time steps and aggregates the hidden
states, and a two-layer fully-connected classifier with Layer Normalization and dropout
produces the class logits. The full model has about 505K trainable parameters.

3.3 Residual Memory Control Cell
A standard LSTM updates its cell memory as ct = ft ⊙ ct−1 + it ⊙ gt . The forget
gate is a simple element-wise multiplication and cannot capture nonlinear interactions
between ct−1 and ht−1 when deciding what to keep. Our HMR cell adds a small
interaction-aware residual gate:

   rt = σ (LayerNorm(Wc ct−1 + Wh ht−1 + mt )),          mt = (Wc ct−1 ) ⊙ (Wh ht−1 )      (2)

Intuitively, rt provides a per-dimension adjustment to the forget gate’s keep/add bal-
ance. The memory is then split into a “keep” stream ckeep   t    = (ft + rt ⊙ (1 − ft )) ⊙ ct−1
and an “add” stream caddt   = (1 − rt ) ⊙ (it ⊙ gt ), which are combined through a learned
softmax attention. Before computing the softmax scores, both ckeep and cadd are layer-
normalised to prevent either stream from dominating by scale alone. The two streams
are then weighted according to their joint content rather than raw memory values.

3.4 Hybrid Memory Path and Temporal Smoothness
Replacing the standard memory update entirely with RMC carries two risks: slow con-
vergence early in training, and stream collapse when the softmax attention saturates.
To avoid both risks, we run the RMC pathway crmc   t   in parallel with the standard
LSTM pathway cLSTMt     and merge them through a learnable blend gate:

                 βt = σ (Wβ ht−1 ),    ct = βt ⊙ crmc
                                                  t   + (1 − βt ) ⊙ cLSTM
                                                                     t                     (3)

The bias of Wβ is initialized to −5, giving βt = σ (−5) ≈ 0.007 at the start of training.
At initialisation, then, the RMC branch is nearly shut off. The network starts training
as a conventional LSTM and only ramps up the residual memory channel if and when
the loss gradients push it to. Because ECG is a continuous signal — not a bag of
independent tokens — we want rt to change smoothly from one time step to the next.
We enforce this with a smoothness penalty:

4 Experimental Protocol
We use the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database [22] in Fazeli’s preprocessed Kaggle ver-
sion, with a stratified 85/15 split (74,420 train, 13,134 validation) and a held-out test
set of 21,892. Each beat is fixed at T = 187 steps and Z-score normalised on training
statistics. The five AAMI EC57 classes are imbalanced 113×: N (82.77%), S (2.54%),
V (6.61%), F (0.73%), Q (7.35%). We compare two classical baselines (Logistic Regres-
sion, Decision Tree), three deep baselines (CNN-LSTM-ATTN, CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN,
and ResNet1D [1, 14]), and HMR-BiLSTM; all deep models share the same CNN
frontend and attention pooling, differing only in the recurrent core, within a compa-
rable 200K–510K parameter range (Transformers are left for future work). All deep
models use Adam (lr 1 × 10−3 , weight decay 1 × 10−4 ), batch size 128, dropout 0.25,
hidden size 96, seed 42; HMR-BiLSTM adds cosine annealing, Focal Loss (γ = 1.5),
and lightweight adversarial training (each step replaces 30% of the batch with FGSM
examples at ε = 0.02), while the baselines use a fixed learning rate, weighted cross-
entropy with the same class weights, and clean data only. We assume a white-box
adversary holding the model weights who can craft bounded L∞ perturbations but
cannot retrain the model; the tested threat is input perturbation to flip predictions,
under FGSM/PGD (Section 5.3) and Gaussian noise (Section 5.4), while data poi-
soning, model extraction, and membership inference are acknowledged but not tested.
We report Accuracy, macro Precision/Recall/F1, weighted F1, and macro AUC, with
macro metrics primary, plus ECE and Brier for calibration. We test three hypotheses:
H1, sequential models beat classical classifiers on temporally structured input; H2,
HMR-BiLSTM improves over CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN, with the Residual Memory Con-
trol and blend gate as the source; H3, HMR-BiLSTM detects rare classes better and
degrades more gracefully under perturbation.

As detailed in Table 1, classical methods fall apart on the minority classes (Logistic
Regression macro F1 0.4787, Decision Tree 0.6846) while every neural model holds
macro F1 above 0.86 and AUC above 0.98, supporting H1. Among the neural models,
HMR-BiLSTM takes the top macro recall (0.9146), macro F1 (0.8825), and AUC

BiLSTM-ATTN. ResNet1D is a strong CNN baseline, beating both recurrent attention
models on accuracy and AUC (0.9736, 0.9914), yet HMR-BiLSTM still edges it on
the two metrics that matter for rare beats (macro F1 by 0.78 pp, macro recall by 0.90
pp), which need temporal context across the whole heartbeat; precision slips slightly
(0.8562 vs 0.8610) but buys the higher recall you want in cardiac monitoring, where

5.3 Adversarial Robustness under FGSM and PGD
We test adversarial robustness with FGSM and PGD-20 (20 iterations, α = 0.005,
random start, L∞ projection) at budgets ε ∈ {0, 0.01, 0.02, 0.05} on Z-score normalised
inputs; at ε = 0.02 the perturbation is visually imperceptible. Under FGSM (Fig. 1),
HMR-BiLSTM holds macro F1 at 0.842 at ε = 0.02 and 0.783 at ε = 0.05, versus
0.773 and 0.683 for CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN; under PGD-20 the gap is similar (0.839 vs
0.756, widening to 0.745 vs 0.573). Since HMR-BiLSTM is trained with adversarial
examples and the baselines are not, this margin reflects the joint effect of the RMC
architecture and adversarial training, and the No-Adv ablation (Table 3) attributes
most of it to the training. Clinically, per-class recall matters more than aggregate F1,
since an attack can selectively erase rare classes: at ε = 0.05 under FGSM (Fig. 2),
HMR-BiLSTM loses only 0.08 on Fusion (0.846 → 0.766) and 0.06 on S, against 0.27
and 0.41 on Fusion for CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN and CNN-LSTM-ATTN; under PGD-20
the Fusion drop is 0.05 versus 0.11 for both baselines, so the advantage concentrates
on the rare, high-risk classes where HMR-BiLSTM already leads on clean data.

5.4 Noise Robustness
For noise robustness (Fig. 3), all models hold steady at σ ≤ 0.05. From σ = 0.3 the
separation becomes clear: HMR-BiLSTM keeps F1 at 0.4798 while CNN-BiLSTM-
ATTN and CNN-LSTM-ATTN collapse to 0.3451 and 0.3507. At σ = 0.4 the margin
grows to 0.3620 versus 0.2386 for CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN. A more complex architecture
normally degrades faster under heavy noise, not slower; HMR-BiLSTM inverts this,
which may reflect implicit input smoothing by the residual gate, though we do not
test this directly.

5.5 Calibration
HMR-BiLSTM has a higher ECE than the baselines (0.0397 vs 0.0056 for CNN-LSTM-
ATTN and 0.0080 for CNN-BiLSTM-ATTN). Top-label ECE is dominated by the
majority class on an imbalanced set, so a model confident on the 82% Normal beats
looks well-calibrated even if its minority-class probabilities are unreliable. Brier score,
which penalises the full probability vector rather than only the top label, reverses the
ranking: 0.0444 for HMR-BiLSTM versus 0.0461 and 0.0542. The higher ECE likely
reflects the combined effect of Focal Loss and adversarial training, which reshape and
broaden the predictive distribution. Neither metric alone captures calibration on a 5-
class imbalanced task; classwise ECE would be more informative but is not reported
here. We do not claim a calibration advantage for HMR-BiLSTM; the evidence is
mixed.

5.6 Interpretability Analysis
Figure 4 shows that the residual gate rt averages to about 0.500 across time steps due
to the LayerNorm–sigmoid architecture and does not vary at the individual-beat level,
so its contribution is a memory-retention bias analysed in Section 5.2 and the clinician-
facing interpretability comes primarily from the attention weights γt . Qualitatively, the
attention distribution shifts with clinical expectations across beat types: for Normal
beats it peaks before the QRS over the P-wave and early QRS that define normal
sinus rhythm; for Ventricular beats it moves to the post-QRS T-wave region where
wide QRS and ST-deviation are most visible; for Fusion beats it concentrates early
(≈ 60 ms) at the pacemaker spike where intrinsic and paced QRS overlap; and for
Supraventricular beats it splits into two peaks near the P-wave and QRS, tracking
the subtle PR-interval differences that separate this class from Normal. In practice, a

6 Discussion and Conclusion
On MIT-BIH, HMR-BiLSTM improves macro F1 and recall over CNN-BiLSTM-
ATTN (by 2.09 and 4.43 pp), concentrated on the clinically significant Supraven-
tricular and Fusion classes, and stays ahead under adversarial and Gaussian-noise
perturbation with a lower Brier score, though none of this amounts to a deployment-
ready claim. Practical and societal impact: the model is best used as a triage aid
on Holter monitors and wearables, flagging suspicious minority-class beats for human
review rather than acting autonomously, while its attention overlay shows which part
of the waveform drove each flag and supports clinician oversight; since such mod-
els can feed clinical decisions, robustness to adversarial and noisy inputs is a safety
and information-security property, not a benchmark nicety. Realising this depends on
responsible deployment: the intra-patient MIT-BIH gains do not establish readiness
across populations or devices, so prospective validation on diverse patient groups and
privacy-preserving training (federated learning, differential privacy) remain prerequi-
sites. Limitations: the intra-patient Kaggle split rather than the inter-patient AAMI
EC57 protocol [20] may give optimistic estimates, and S/F performance stays below
the dominant Normal class; stronger adaptive attacks (Carlini–Wagner, AutoAttack)
and cross-dataset validation on Vietnamese clinical ECG remain for future work.
